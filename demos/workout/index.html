How to progress (tap to read)
  • When you hit the top of the rep range on every set, add the smallest weight jump available next time.
  • Add a set to a muscle only after progress stalls for 2–3 weeks.
  • Volume is 6–10 sets per muscle per week by your choice. That is the low end of the 10–20 range BarBend suggests, and what Dotmoovs calls the minimum effective dose. A trial in trained men found more sets grew more muscle, so add sets if progress stalls.
  • Focus on technique over load for the first six months.
  • Targets use straight sets: the same weight on every set, with a rep goal inside the plan's range. The research behind that: moderate loads build muscle about as well as heavy ones when sets are hard (Schoenfeld 2021, Mitchell 2012), 6–15 reps is the practical range (Iversen 2021), training to failure on every set is not needed but sets should end close to it (Refalo 2023), and ramping weights up across sets is no better than straight sets at equal volume. The starting weight is the Epley estimate for the goal reps with about 2 reps left, so most weights are my estimates (marked "estimate"); adjust them in week 1. Warm-up sets are listed separately and are not counted as working sets.
  • Next week's weight comes from your last set of that exercise, using a rule of my own: that set's weight becomes the weight for every set. If you hit the rep goal on it, the weight rises to what Epley (1-rep max ≈ weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)) says you could lift for the goal reps, at least +5 lb, rounded to 5. If you fall short, you repeat that weight. For example, a last set of 200 lb × 8 against a goal of 8 makes next week 205 lb × 8 on every set. Assisted pull-ups lower the assistance by 5 lb, and bodyweight moves add a rep or a second. Epley gets less accurate above about 12 reps.
  • You don't know your weights yet, so tap Change next to any target in the Workout log to set your own weight or rep goal. It applies to that session and the ones after it, until you log a session on or after that day, and then the last-set rule takes over again. Back to suggested removes it.
  • Each muscle is trained once a week on its own day, with 6–7 sets per muscle per session (about the 6–8 hard sets per session that Weightology describes). The same sources find frequency matters less than weekly volume, with a slight edge for training a muscle twice when volume is high. Use Target day to hit a lagging muscle a second time.
  • Friday is Core. Saturday is a Target day: a free choice of any muscle and exercise. Its sets are not counted in the weekly totals above.
  • If you're sore beyond 72 hours or keep missing days, skip the Target day first.

    How these targets work

    • Calories: maintenance at age 30 is about 2,570 kcal (Mifflin-St Jeor × 1.55, my estimate). The target adds about 500 kcal a day for each pound a week you want to gain, or subtracts the same to lose, a common rule of thumb that isn't from the sources I fetched. For losing, the CDC recommends a gradual pace of about 1 to 2 pounds a week. For comparison, Bony to Beastly suggests a 200–400 kcal surplus for 0.5–1 lb a week and Dr. Feigenbaum suggests 250–500 kcal. Add ~200 kcal if your weekly average hasn't moved in two weeks; cut ~100 if you gain faster than your chosen rate or gain fat.
    • Protein: with normal kidney function, the ISSN range is 1.4–2.0 g/kg, and summaries of the Morton meta-analysis put the benefit plateau near 1.6 g/kg with an upper range near 2.2 g/kg. That research ties protein to body weight, not to how fast you gain, so I stepped the target from 1.6 g/kg at 0.5 lb/week up to 2.2 g/kg at 2 lb/week, inside the supported range. That scaling is my choice. It uses your latest logged weight. When you pick Lose, protein goes up to 2.0–2.6 g/kg: the ISSN position stand on diets and body composition suggests 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass for lean lifters in a deficit, which I converted to body weight assuming about 15% body fat (my estimate). The same paper says slower weight loss keeps more muscle. Large protein doses can raise glucose hours later (see the heavy-meal note below), so watch your CGM after big protein meals.
    • Creatine: 3–5 g a day is the ISSN maintenance dose, the same at every gain rate. It adds a few pounds of water early. That isn't fat.
    • Spread it out: 4–5 meals of roughly 20–35 g protein, every 3–4 hours at consistent times.
    • Carbs and insulin: carbs are the main calorie source for gaining, so you'll dose for more carbs than before. Favor fiber-rich sources and pair them with protein or fat, and keep meal sizes and times consistent so your ratios stay predictable. Fat is the other calorie lever.
    • Insulin needs will probably shift as you eat more and gain weight (my inference, not from a source), and faster at higher gain rates. Plan an endocrinology check-in at 4–6 weeks to review basal, carb ratios and corrections rather than adjusting on your own.

    Carb budget

    Meal examples

    Totals are summed from the table above (plus 5 g for vegetables where listed). Protein shown is from the foods counted.

    Breakfast · 56–61 g carbs
    • 1 cup cooked oatmeal + 1 small apple + 1 cup whole milk + 3 scrambled eggs = 56 g carbs · 33 g protein
    • 3 slices whole-wheat toast + ½ banana + 3 eggs + 1 cup raw peppers or spinach = 61 g carbs · 31 g protein
    • ¾ cup plain yogurt + 1½ cups Cheerios-type cereal + ½ banana (plus a scoop of whey, not counted) = 57 g carbs · 15 g protein
    Lunch · 67–70 g carbs
    • Chicken burrito bowl: 1 cup white rice + ½ cup black beans + 4 oz chicken breast + 1 cup raw veg = 70 g carbs · 47 g protein
    • Turkey wrap: 2 small flour tortillas + ½ cup chickpeas + 1 orange + 4 oz turkey = 68 g carbs · 47 g protein
    • Beef pasta: 1⅓ cups cooked pasta + 4 oz ground beef + 1 small apple + side salad = 67 g carbs · 39 g protein
    Dinner · 75–86 g carbs
    • Salmon (4 oz) + 1 cup sweet potato + 1 cup quinoa + cooked greens = 86 g carbs · 37 g protein
    • Chicken breast (4 oz) + 1 cup lentils + ⅔ cup white rice + ½ cup cooked veg = 75 g carbs · 56 g protein
    • Beef stir-fry (4 oz beef) + 1⅓ cups white rice + 1 orange = 80 g carbs · 36 g protein
    Snacks · 25–34 g carbs
    • Peanut butter sandwich: 2 slices whole-wheat bread + 1 tbsp peanut butter = 31 g carbs · 12 g protein
    • ¾ cup plain yogurt + ¼ cup granola = 29 g carbs · 14 g protein
    • 1 banana + 2 tbsp peanut butter = 34 g carbs · 8 g protein
    • Shake: 1 cup whole milk + ½ banana (plus a scoop of whey, not counted) = 25 g carbs · 8 g protein
    Around training
    • Before: whether you need carbs depends on your CGM value and arrow. A diabetes-nutrition source suggests pairing carbs with protein or fat, such as a banana with peanut butter.
    • After: a shake with 1 cup whole milk and a whole banana (39 g carbs, 9 g protein) plus whey, then a normal meal. Delayed lows can follow training, so ask your team about the bedtime plan.
    • For a low: ½ cup juice is 13–14 g of fast carbs. Glucose tablets also work.
    Fat sources and heavy meals

    Fat: olive oil, nuts and nut butter, avocado, cheese, fatty fish, whole eggs. These are common choices, not from the sources above. Protein foods are in the table above.

    Heavy meals and your pump: an NHS leaflet says meals over 30 g fat or 40 g protein can cause a delayed glucose rise. Its dosing advice (about +20% on the carb bolus, split 60/40 over ~3 hours) is for pumps that aren't closed-loop and hasn't been tested on them, so don't copy it. Tandem says Control-IQ+ supports an extended bolus of up to 8 hours that can help with high-fat or high-protein meals, but no source I found gives a standard upfront/extended split. A 2026 study in adolescents on Control-IQ found 6-hour time in range was lower after a high-fat meal (65%) and a high-protein meal (50%) than after a standard meal (84%) with regular boluses. Work out your split with your team.

    Before you start: tell your endocrinology team you're starting a lifting program and changing how much you eat. If you use an insulin pump or CGM, ask for: your personal pre-workout range, when to switch Exercise mode on, whether your carb ratio or correction factor should change on training days, how long after a mealtime bolus to wait before lifting, how to handle overnight lows after training, and your ketone rules.

    Around each workout

    1. 30–60 minutes before, note your CGM value and trend arrow. Confirm with a fingerstick if you feel off or the reading doesn't match.
    2. The 2017 type 1 consensus (Riddell et al.) suggests starting around 126–180 mg/dL for aerobic exercise up to an hour. It says the start target should be individualized, and lifting can raise glucose temporarily, so your range may differ.
    3. Low or falling: below ~90 mg/dL, sources suggest 15–30 g of fast carbs and a recheck in ~15 min. A table tested in adolescents lists ~8 g at 109–124 mg/dL with a falling arrow and ~20 g below 90 with a falling arrow. Adults may need more.
    4. High: lifting can push glucose up temporarily. A summary of the position statement has pre-exercise correction advice for pumps, but it doesn't apply to closed-loop systems like yours, so follow your team's plan. Check ketones if you're high and unwell.
    5. Set your CGM low alert, keep fast carbs on you, and stop if you drop.
    6. Check after, at bedtime and the next morning, with overnight low alerts on. Delayed lows can show up 6–15 hours after training, and insulin sensitivity stays raised for 24–48 hours.

    What to expect

    • The consensus says aerobic exercise generally lowers glucose, anaerobic work like lifting can raise it temporarily, and both can cause delayed lows during recovery. Your CGM history from the first few weeks will show your own pattern.
    • A small 2012 Diabetes Care study (12 people with type 1) found resistance work before aerobic work kept glucose steadier. Do lifting first if you add cardio.
    • Your pump (Tandem Mobi, Control-IQ+): per Tandem's quick reference, Exercise (Actions menu) raises the treatment target to 140–160 mg/dL and stops basal when predicted glucose reaches 80. Control-IQ must be on with an active CGM session, and it can still deliver an automatic correction once an hour while Exercise is on. Tandem says Control-IQ doesn't prevent all highs and lows and isn't a substitute for active management. I didn't find a Mobi-specific recommendation for when to switch it on before lifting, so ask your team.
    • Resistance training 2–3 times a week is recommended for people with type 1, alongside at least 150 minutes of weekly activity, with no more than 2 days in a row off.

    Not medical advice. The 2017 consensus is the main source, but I couldn't open its full text, so numbers come from summaries, one 12-person study and a table tested in adolescents. Confirm all thresholds, insulin changes and ketone rules with your endocrinology team.
